# No Code Alex — Camera-Direction Prompt Pack

Six shot recipes + a reusable brief for the Claude video workflow.

Use this alongside the video's skill file. These are creative directions, not guaranteed model controls. Different models may interpret the same wording differently. The skill can propose a camera move; use the recipes when you want to specify your own vision.

## Start with one clear shot

Copy this into your configured Claude video workflow. Replace the bracketed fields before generating.

```text
Create one continuous shot of [subject] doing [one action] in [location].
Begin on [starting composition].
Camera: [one move from a recipe below].
End on [specific final composition].
Keep [subject identity, clothing, prop or location details] consistent.
Mood and light: [short description].
Duration: [supported duration]. Aspect ratio: [supported format].
Before generating, show the proposed model and estimated cost and ask me to confirm.
```

Ask for supported settings instead of assuming every model accepts every duration or control. An estimate or budget in a prompt is not a provider-enforced spending cap. Confirm pricing and review actual charges. Never paste a secret API key into a public prompt or shared video.

## 1. Push in — build attention

Best when the subject stays in one place and the viewer should notice a detail.

```text
Start in a medium-wide shot of [subject]. Move the camera slowly forward toward
[important detail], keeping the subject centered. Let the environment move
naturally in perspective. Settle into a close view at the end. One continuous
shot; no cuts, abrupt acceleration, or sudden digital zoom.
```

Example: a ceramic artist places the final piece on a workbench; the camera moves toward the glaze texture.

## 2. Orbit — show form and depth

Best for a mostly stationary subject with a clear silhouette.

```text
Frame [subject] at [height]. Move through a gentle quarter-circle around it,
keeping the subject the same size in frame. Reveal [background detail] as the
angle changes. Finish at [end angle]. One continuous move; keep the subject's
shape and identity consistent.
```

Example: a person in a raincoat stands beneath a station canopy as the camera reveals the arriving train behind them.

## 3. Follow — make an action readable

Best for walking, running or a simple physical action.

```text
Follow [subject] from [behind / alongside] as they [one action]. Keep their
body and the destination readable in frame. Match the camera speed to the
subject. Show the complete action, including its landing or stopping point.
Settle with the subject rather than ending mid-movement. No cuts or teleporting.
```

Example: follow a person approaching and jumping a short rooftop gap; keep the takeoff, gap and landing visible. Start with a simpler action if continuity breaks.

## 4. Reveal — turn context into a payoff

Best when the setting itself is the surprise.

```text
Begin with [foreground object] obscuring part of the view. Move the camera
slowly sideways past it to reveal [subject or setting]. Finish with the reveal
fully visible and hold briefly. Keep the move in one direction and the shot
continuous. No unrelated objects appearing during the reveal.
```

Example: move past a doorway to reveal a sunlit studio full of finished paintings.

## 5. Lock off — let the subject do the moving

Best when a stable frame helps viewers compare motion or inspect detail.

```text
Keep the camera fixed in a [wide / medium / close] composition. [Subject]
performs [one action] within the frame. Keep the background and framing stable.
Finish with the result of the action visible. No camera pan, zoom or cut.
```

Example: a barista pours milk into a cup; the fixed overhead view lets the pattern form on screen.

## 6. Crane up — reveal scale

Best when the wider environment matters to the story.

```text
Start near [subject] at [height]. Raise the camera smoothly while widening the
view to reveal [environment]. Keep the subject identifiable as the setting
opens up. Finish on a composed wide view. One continuous movement, no sudden
perspective jumps and no rapid spinning.
```

Example: rise above a person at a quiet coastal lookout to reveal the cliffs and ocean beyond.

## Refine without losing the idea

1. Keep the same subject, action and starting frame.
2. Change one camera instruction at a time.
3. Compare framing, camera movement, subject continuity and the ending—not just sharpness.
4. If the action breaks, simplify it before adding more camera movement.
5. Save the prompt, model, settings and actual cost with the result.

Useful follow-up:

```text
Keep the subject, action, duration and visual style. Change only the camera:
[replacement direction]. Explain the change and show the estimate before
generating. Do not generate additional variants without my confirmation.
```

## A fair A/B comparison

Use the same prompt, duration, aspect ratio and model/settings where possible.
Record any differences. Compare the original files at normal playback speed.
A monthly subscription and a per-generation charge are different units;
neither alone proves that two services are equivalent.
