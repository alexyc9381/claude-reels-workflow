# Why these animation choices work

Living direction, derived from Alex's repeated Higgsfield revisions through V23 (September 12, 2026). Read with [current direction](CURRENT-DIRECTION.md), [visual-first explainers](VISUAL-FIRST-EXPLAINERS.md) and the [revision ledger](REVISION-DECISIONS.md). Later explicit feedback wins. Alex said the V22 animations were “a lot better”; that validates the direction, not every future scene or a measured retention improvement.

## 1. Narration supplies the nouns, verbs, quantity and order

Write those four things before designing. “Generate hundreds of videos” needs visible accumulation, not one output plus a decorative label. “Create, copy, keep safe” needs three different changes of state in that order, timed to the actual words. Approximate review timestamps locate a passage; source words and rendered playback set the final cue.

V23 example: the direct-model machine grows a contact sheet of 240 illustrated thumbnails while the narration says hundreds. The count is an illustration of scale, **not** a measured output-per-dollar benchmark. No exact count is advertised on-screen. The privacy sequence follows Settings → API keys → add/create → copy → private storage; it is not a single card with changing headlines.

## 2. Simple concept, detailed action

Complexity belongs in meaningful mechanics, not more unrelated objects. A character approaches, presses, lifts, transfers and receives; the destination reacts on contact and the scene ends changed. Gripped props share the carrier's path. Reaction settles instead of stopping abruptly. An extra sprite earns its place through a distinct job, not idle duplication.

V22's two grounded cost bases and cross-stage rivalry were clearer than scattered command/model/price fragments. Preserve that structure when adding logos: reserve a common strip, avoid covering the produced result, and keep prices attached to their bases.

## 3. Recognition beats paragraphs

Use real model/company marks with short labels when identity is the idea. Retain enough text for accessibility and unfamiliar viewers; less text does not mean unlabeled ambiguity. A row of familiar marks can establish “standard models” quickly without a paragraph. Do not redraw a company's mark, claim an editorial mascot is official, or treat a logo lineup as verified universal compatibility.

## 4. Hierarchy is temporal as well as spatial

One dominant action leads; secondary movement supports it. Fast anticipation/contact can coexist with enough dwell to understand the result. Never make readable text shake. A literal physical roadmap, stopwatch, key, camera or folder needs recognizability and functional motion, not an inventory of arbitrary rectangles. Reuse materials and character rigs; don't repeat the same whole explanatory scene.

## 5. Brand color must preserve separation

Terracotta Claude disappears on saturated orange. Use stronger orange near the frame edges, a paler warm field behind actors and a fine dark silhouette rim. Keep the original character hue. White glass needs readable dark text, controlled highlights and uncluttered contents; adding glow cannot repair a poor layout. Check the actual video frame at thumbnail size, not only a zoomed screenshot.

The 10¢ correction is a reusable typography rule: one fixed-width group, explicit line heights, smaller approximation symbol, centered unit beneath, and clearance from curved edges and facecam. Pricing units remain different (monthly vs estimated per generation); polish is not claim verification.

## 6. The face is important; evidence remains primary

V23 raises each inset's width/height by 20% (44% more area), keeping the source crop and bottom anchor. This is not a 20% tighter face crop. Recheck cards, logos, sprite feet and typing at peak overlap. Opening full-size face → inset stays; A/B footage remains large. Do not enlarge a waiting face over the result playback.

For an awkward visual expression over useful speech, cut away to the real screen while keeping the voice. Trim only source-confirmed empty time. Never repair a look-away by clipping the last word. One cumulative frame timeline controls all following cues and chapters.

## 7. Privacy is a security boundary and a teaching opportunity

Tell the viewer why the real screen is unavailable. Real keys remain behind an opaque mask; a blur alone is not adequate secret protection. Synthetic masked bullets, an illustrated key and a storage metaphor teach the operation without reproducing the secret. Keep the key-workflow explanation on one surface, with one active step, and cover transitions too. Avoid promising that a copied key is automatically secured by the actual software.

## 8. Sound has a job, and claims have limits

Countdown clicks predict a real editorial event (result/reaction/reveal), not model processing time. A restrained bed can make a deliberately retained nonverbal playback feel intentional. Movement/landing accents follow the clip, and music falls away for the reaction. Preserve OBS as the only recorded narration; music and effects remain separately labeled design tracks. Reuse ledgered sources, retain attribution, and measure the final encoded audio. A meter is not a listening approval.

Do not add fake sound to a comparison and present it as model-generated evidence. Record editorial sound additions in the revision/provenance notes. Keep actual-result playback unobstructed where possible.

## Review checklist

- Can an audio-off viewer identify the action and changed ending?
- Do the visible quantity, identities and step order match the words?
- Is the biggest thing the most important thing at that moment?
- Are moving hands, props and receivers spatially coupled?
- Does every added object have an explanatory job?
- Do text, sprite silhouettes and price units survive a small preview?
- Are credentials protected for the entire interval, including seams?
- Does the final movie contain the actual changes, not just a source patch or intro preview?
- Are render checks, creative feedback and publication/claim clearance distinguished?
